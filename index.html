TGAT1 & A-Level Focus

Discourse Markers Rules & Categories

Discourse markers are words or phrases used to organize speech or writing, connect ideas, or guide the reader.

➕ Addition (เพิ่มเติม)

also, furthermore, in addition

"In addition, you can choose the movie."

⚡ Contrast (ขัดแย้ง)

however, but, although, on the other hand

"However, do not use the phone."

💡 Explanation (อธิบาย)

in other words, that is to say

"In other words, he likes simplicity."

🔄 Topic Change (เปลี่ยนเรื่อง)

by the way, anyway, now, moving on

"By the way, have you seen the rules?"

Grammar Core Rule

Bare Infinitives (V.1 ไม่เติม to / -ing / -ed)

A bare infinitive is the base form of a verb without "to". In English grammar (and TGAT/A-Level error identification), bare infinitives MUST be used in 4 major structures:

1. Modal Verbs

can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must

You should study more.

2. Causative Verbs

make, let, help

They made me wait outside.

3. Perception Verbs

see, hear, feel, watch, notice

I saw him run down the street.

4. Fixed Expressions

had better, would rather

You had better take off shoes.

Unit 5 Reading • p. 65

Fitting into a New Culture

💡 Interactive Feature: Click on any highlighted cyan word to view its vocabulary definition and Thai translation instantly!

When I first arrived in America, I was pretty reserved. I couldn't help but wonder if I'd fit in. One of the first things I noticed was the number of different languages, accents, styles, and expressions. It made me realize how diverse this country is. That diversity makes each day a learning opportunity. It's one of the most fascinating aspects of living here.

Aside from generally being outgoing, American students are involved in lots of activities. It feels like there's a club for everything! A few weeks into the semester, our school hosted a club showcase on campus. The digital media club booth caught my eye. They had colorful posters, cameras, and a green screen set up. I imagined how fun it would be to learn video editing or voice acting. I picked up the sign-up sheet but paused. Suddenly, I felt a wave of uncertainty. I barely knew anything about video production. I started to feel overwhelmed. What if I wasn't good enough? What if there was another club better suited for me?

That's when someone approached me with the warmest smile. She said, "Konnichiwa!"—that's "hello" in Japanese. It instantly reminded me of home, and I smiled back. Her name was Amber, and she was the president of the digital media club. She had spent two years studying abroad in Osaka and knew exactly how it felt to be dropped into a new culture. "When in Rome, do as the Romans do," she told me as we sat down together at a café later that afternoon. I blinked. "So... does that mean I should act like everyone else?" Amber nodded. "Kind of. It means being open to the way things are done here... you know, giving things a try, even if they're unfamiliar."

I thought for a moment. It didn't mean losing my identity; it meant adapting and maybe even discovering new parts of myself along the way. That lunch turned into a long conversation. We talked about how complicated and beautiful living in another culture can be. Amber told me stories about her time in Japan, like how she got lost in translation in a rural town because she misunderstood a train schedule. We laughed a lot.

As we walked back across campus, I realized something: I didn't need to have everything figured out. Being part of a diverse community meant that others were figuring things out, too. Joining the club could help me grow into someone more confident, expressive, and outgoing. So, I signed up, and I'm really enjoying it!
